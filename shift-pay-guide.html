Shift pay basics

How to track shifts and estimate gross pay

A reliable estimate starts with the minutes you worked and the exact pay rules that applied. This guide explains the inputs to collect before comparing an estimate with your paycheck.

Start with the source inputs

Record each shift's start and end time, the unpaid break, and whether you actually worked the shift. Keep base rate, overtime policy, differential windows, differential amounts, and fixed bonuses separate so you can trace the estimate later.

Do not put a take-home amount into a gross-pay comparison. Gross pay is the amount before taxes and deductions; net or take-home pay is what remains after deductions.

Use a transparent base formula

For a simple shift without overtime or premiums, the estimate is paid hours multiplied by the base hourly rate. Paid hours equal elapsed shift time minus the unpaid break.

Fictional example: A 12-hour shift with a 30-minute unpaid break has 11.5 paid hours. At a fictional base rate of $30 per hour, the base gross estimate is $345 before any overtime, differential, or bonus.

Real pay policies may round time or place breaks differently. Match the employer's written policy rather than assuming every workplace uses the same method.

Treat overtime as a rule, not a guess

Overtime can depend on the applicable jurisdiction, workweek boundaries, employer policy, contract terms, and the worker's classification. A calculator should identify which preset or rule is being estimated and avoid presenting an estimate as official payroll.

Keep the overtime portion visible in the breakdown. If the final gross differs, a separate line makes it easier to see whether the possible difference comes from hours, rate, or premium treatment.

Map night and weekend differentials

A shift differential may be a fixed amount per eligible hour or a multiplier. Eligibility can depend on a time window, day of week, holiday status, role, or employer policy.

Overnight shifts require special care because a time window can cross midnight. Track the eligible minutes, not just the date on which the shift began.

Compare expected and actual gross

Compare the estimate with the actual gross amount for the same employer and pay period. If the two numbers differ, first verify the pay-period dates, worked shifts, unpaid breaks, base rate, overtime rule, differentials, and bonuses.

A numerical difference is a signal to review, not proof of underpayment. Payroll can include policy details or corrections that an estimator does not know.

Know the limits

A shift-pay estimator is not a payroll system, tax calculator, legal opinion, or financial advisor. Preserve the assumptions behind each completed check and verify important results using official employer documentation.
